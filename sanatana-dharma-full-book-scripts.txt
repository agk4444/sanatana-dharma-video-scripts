SANATANA DHARMA — FULL-BOOK VIDEO SCRIPTS
Spoken narration for all 24 chapters, as recorded for the video.
Source: Sanatana Dharma: An Elementary Text-book of Hindu Religion and Ethics (1916).

CHAPTER 1 — The One Existence
=============================

Segment 1
Chapter One. The One Existence. The first chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, opens with a single idea, and everything else in the book grows out of it.

Segment 2
There is one Infinite, Eternal, Changeless Existence, the All. From That, all comes forth; to That, all returns. The book gives the image of a wave in the ocean. As a wave rises in the ocean and sinks again into it, so a universe rises in the All and sinks again into the All. As the ocean is water and the wave is a form of the water, so there is one Existence, and the universe is a form of it. One only, without a second. The name given to the All in this book is Brahman. English-speaking people use the name God.

Segment 3
Brahman has two states. Unmanifested, without attributes, He is called Nirguna Brahman. Manifested, with attributes, He is Saguna Brahman, the Supreme Ishvara, with His universe. With Himself as Spirit, Ishvara reveals the other side of the All: Mulaprakriti, the Root of Matter. Matter is that which takes form. Everything we can touch, taste, smell, see and hear is Matter. But the whole of it is not Matter. In each thing, inaudible and invisible, there is Spirit, a portion of Ishvara. The Matter part is called the body, the sheath, the vehicle. The Spirit in a body is called the Jiva, a separated Self.

Segment 4
Spirit and Matter are the first pair of opposites, and the book insists we never confuse them. Spirit is life. It thinks, feels and observes. It is the I in each of us, and it is one and the same in everybody and in everything. Matter cannot think, or feel, or observe. It is without consciousness, and it tends always to divide itself into many forms. Spirit has three qualities: Being, Thought-Power and Bliss. Matter has three as well: Inertia, Mobility and Rhythm. Inertia gives stability, Mobility keeps Matter moving, and Rhythm makes the movements regular. Even a stone, the book says, is vibrating, every particle in it moving rapidly to and fro.

Segment 5
What makes Matter begin to take form is Shakti, the Divine Power of Ishvara, called Maya. Ishvara is the Lord of Maya. And the chapter closes with the words of the Bhagavad Gita: Brahman is everywhere, with hands and feet everywhere, eyes, heads and mouths everywhere, unattached, supporting everything, seated in the hearts of all. That is the One Existence. And from it, the book will go on to the Many, to rebirth, to karma, and to sacrifice.


CHAPTER 2 — The Many
====================

Segment 1
Chapter Two. The Many. The second chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, asks how the One becomes the Many. The book's answer is that the Many are not separate from the One; they are His faces, His powers, and His children. The One, the Supreme Ishvara, appears as Three, and from those Three come the worlds, the senses, the Devas, and men.

Segment 2
When Ishvara shines out on Prakriti and makes it fall into shapes, the first Forms that appear are the Trimurti, the three Aspects of Ishvara. Brahma is the Creator, who shapes matter into the seven Tattvas, the Elements: Pure Reason and Egoism, then Ether, Air, Fire, Water, and Earth. Vishnu is the Preserver, who maintains the worlds. Shiva, or Mahadeva, is the Dissolver, who dissolves the worlds when they are worn out and of no further use. These are the first manifestations of Ishvara, His Supreme Forms, His three Aspects, or Faces.

Segment 3
In everything there are three gunas, three qualities, never separated, though one may dominate. When Sattva dominates, the being is called sattvik; when Rajas dominates, rajasik; when Tamas dominates, tamasik. The Elements are mostly tamasik, dull and inert. The ten Indriyas, the five senses and the five powers of action, with Manah, the mind, are mostly rajasik, very active. The Devas, the ministers of Ishvara who administer His laws and see that each man gets what he has earned by his karma, are mostly sattvik. Ishvara is the King, the One Lord, and the Devas are His ministers, like the ministers and officials of an earthly king.

Segment 4
Brahma then created in His mind minerals, plants, animals and men, and this world-evolution, called Samsara, is compared to a wheel, constantly turning, on which all Jivas are bound. Vishnu breathed His Life into all these forms and became Prana in all forms, and the whole universe became full of life and consciousness. But the life of Mahadeva must also be poured out, to complete the triple Jiva of man, that he might be the perfect reflection of the triple Ishvara. And Vishnu comes down again and again as an Avatara, One who descends, whenever things are going badly with the world. The Ten Avataras mark the great stages of evolution: Matsya the Fish, Kurma the Tortoise, Varaha the Boar, Narasimha the Man-Lion, Vamana the Dwarf, Parashurama, Rama the Perfect Man, Krishna, Buddha, and Kalki, who shall close the Kali Yuga.

Segment 5
The chapter closes with the words of the sages. The Rigveda says: Of Him who is one, sages speak as manifold. And the Upanishad gives the image of a blazing fire, from which sparks, all similar to each other, spring forth in thousands, so from the Indestructible, various types of being are born, and they also return thither. And the Bhagavad Gita promises: When dharma decays, when adharma is exalted, then I Myself come forth, for the protection of the good, for firmly establishing dharma, I am born from age to age. Next: Chapter 3 — Re-birth.


CHAPTER 3 — Re-birth
====================

Segment 1
Chapter Three. Re-birth. The third chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, takes up the evolution of the last chapter and shows how it happens: the Jiva passes from body to body, the bodies improving as his powers unfold. Re-incarnation means taking flesh again. Transmigration means passing into a new body.

Segment 2
The Jiva, the book says, is a portion of Brahman. Shri Krishna calls him "a portion of myself, a Jiva," and the Shruti teaches, "Thou art That." The book gives the image of a seed and a tree. A tree drops a seed carrying its own nature, and the seed grows until it becomes a tree like its parent. So the Jiva is dropped into matter by Ishvara, and grows, putting out his hidden powers, until he becomes Ishvara. His pilgrimage begins in the mineral kingdom, unconscious of the outer world, until earthquakes, volcanoes and the furious surf awaken him. Then he passes into plants, feeling the warm sun and the rain, and then into animals, where hunting and outwitting bring out his senses, until no animal form is good enough, and he needs the human.

Segment 3
The student may ask: what makes the forms evolve to suit the Jiva? The Jiva's own efforts. He wants to look out through the wall of matter that encloses him. He tries to see, and his outward-going energy slowly, very slowly, evolves an eye. The senses, the book says, are pierced from within outwards. The Devas help him, giving him materials suited to the organ he wants: Agni gives fiery matter for sight, Varuna watery matter for taste. When he has shaped a body as much as he can, and it is of no further use to him, he throws it away and makes another, and he evolves faster and faster as he goes on.

Segment 4
The stream is onwards, but there are twists and turnings. A Jiva may slip backwards for a time, to a stage he has long left behind, like a boy at school put back into a lower class. A Jiva who has reached the human stage may even be attached to an animal, a plant, or a stone, until he has learned to use the human form better, like a man in prison. But he is not tied to the wheel of births and deaths for ever. The ropes that tie him to the wheel are his desires. So long as he desires the objects of this earth, he must come back to enjoy them. When he ceases to desire them, the ropes are broken, and he is free. He is then called a Mukta, a free Jiva, and often such Muktas remain to help others get free sooner.

Segment 5
The chapter closes with the words of the Bhagavad Gita. "As a man, casting off worn-out garments, taketh new ones, so the dweller in the body, casting off worn-out bodies, entereth into others that are new." As a goldsmith takes gold and makes a form new and more beautiful, so the Atma makes another form. He who acts placing all his actions in Brahman, abandoning attachment, is unaffected by sin, as a lotus leaf by the waters. And he who is happy within, rejoicing within, illuminated within, goes to the Nirvana of Brahman. Next: Chapter 4 — Karma.


CHAPTER 4 — Karma
=================

Segment 1
Chapter Four. Karma. The fourth chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, takes up a single Sanskrit word — Karma — which means action. But here it means something more: the definite connection between what we do now and what will happen in the future. Nothing happens by accident, the book says. Everything follows in regular order.

Segment 2
The book begins with a seed. Plant a seed, and it sends up a stem; leaves grow, flowers come, then fruits, and in the fruits are seeds again. The same seed always gives the same plant: rice gives rice, wheat gives wheat, thistle gives thistle. Sow thistles, and you must not expect sweet grapes; plant prickly cactus, and you must not expect juicy apples. This is karma: knowing it, a man sows the seed of the thing he wants to reap. And an action is not so simple as it looks. Behind every action stands a thought, and behind every thought a desire. Desire, thought, action — three threads twisted into the cord of karma.

Segment 3
Whatever we sow by our actions comes back to us. If we make the people around us happy, we have sown happiness, and it grows up into happiness for ourselves. If we make them unhappy, we have sown unhappiness, and it returns as unhappiness for ourselves. But thought stands behind action, and thought makes our character. As we think, so we become: think kindly and we become kind; think cruelly and we become cruel. And when we are born again, we shall be born with the character our thoughts are making now. Behind thought stands desire. As a magnet attracts soft iron, so desire attracts objects: desire learning, and we shall have the chance to become learned in another life; desire love, and the chance of becoming loved.

Segment 4
But are we then helplessly bound? The chapter says: true, but only to a certain extent. We are always changing, as we gain new experiences, and the Jiva gains more knowledge and changes his desires. A man who has acted cruelly sees that his cruelty made others hate and fear him, and made him lonely and unhappy. He resolves to change, and goes to the root of the trouble — the desire itself. Instead of letting desires carry him away like runaway horses, he uses thought as a bridle, and keeps his desires in check. Liberation is not won by fleeing to the forest. King Janaka kept his kingdom and wealth, yet said that if all Mithila burned, nothing would be lost to him. The merchant Tuladhara reached the same freedom by harming none, asking no loan, and quarrelling with none.

Segment 5
The chapter closes with the old scriptures. Man verily is desire-formed, says the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad: as is his desire, so goes his thought; as his thought is, so he does action; as he does action, so he attains. And the Katha Upanishad gives the image of the chariot: the Self is the chariot-owner, Reason the charioteer, the mind the reins, the senses the horses. With mind tightened, the senses are controlled, like good horses of the charioteer. That is karma: we reap as we sow, and we are free to sow anew. Next: Chapter 5 — Sacrifice.


CHAPTER 5 — Sacrifice
=====================

Segment 1
Chapter Five. Sacrifice. In the fifth chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, the idea of offering sacrifices, so familiar in India, is traced back to its root, so that we may see what sacrifice really means. The chapter shows that the whole of life, from stone to saint, runs on one law: life poured out for the benefit of others.

Segment 2
The first thing to grasp is that creation is sacrifice. Ishvara confines Himself, limits Himself, in matter, in order that a universe may be made manifest. Immersion in matter is, in spiritual language, called death, and Ishvara thus sacrificed Himself to bring into separate being portions of Himself, the Jivas, who might develop all His powers in matter. This is the primary sacrifice, and on this is based the Law. Sacrifice, then, is the pouring out of life for the benefit of others.

Segment 3
The Law of Sacrifice is the law of life for all Jivas. In the earlier stages they are forcibly sacrificed, and so progress involuntarily. The mineral is broken up for the support of plants, the vegetable for the support of animal life, the animal for the support of other animals and of men. Only after untold ages does the Jiva recognise the universality of the law, and begin to sacrifice deliberately for the good of those around him. This is called self-sacrifice, and it is the showing forth of the divinity of the Jiva, the proof that he is of the nature of Ishvara.

Segment 4
The Mahabharata tells a wonderful story of uttermost self-sacrifice. Indra, king of the Devas, was defeated by the Asura Vritra, and could only be saved by a thunderbolt made from the self-given bones of a holy one. The Rishi Dadhichi was filled with great pity and said, "I give to you my body willingly, to make what use of it you like." His bones were taken, and Vritra fell before the might born of that wondrous sacrifice. The Rishis lead the Jiva to this point step by step, teaching him first to sacrifice his possessions, and to deny himself present enjoyments, in the hope of reward, until the practice of sacrificing becomes habitual.

Segment 5
Another lesson of these sacrifices is that all lives are inter-dependent. The Rishis taught man to sacrifice daily to the Devas, to Rishis, to ancestors, to men and animals, for as all these enrich his life, he owes them a debt, which he must pay by sacrifice. As he lives on others, he must, in common honesty, live for others. At last sacrifice becomes happiness, a delight. He tries to see how little he can take and how much he can give, and to become the friend of all creatures. Then every action becomes a sacrifice to Ishvara, and actions no longer bind him. Thus the law of sacrifice becomes also the law of liberation. Next: Chapter 6 — The Worlds, Visible and Invisible.


CHAPTER 6 — The Worlds — Visible and Invisible
==============================================

Segment 1
Chapter Six. The Worlds — Visible and Invisible. The sixth chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, turns from the One Existence to the worlds the Jiva travels through on its long pilgrimage. We know the world our senses show us. But the book says there are other worlds too, invisible ones, where the Jiva goes after death, and in these three worlds its evolution proceeds.

Segment 2
We all know one world, the world around us, which we can see and hear and touch and taste and smell. But science itself tells us of parts of this world our senses are too dull to perceive, things too small for our eyes to see. These parts are still physical, although invisible to us. And beyond them, the book says, there are other worlds, invisible and not a part of this one: the worlds into which people go when they pass away from this earth by death. These are the Triloka, the three worlds.

Segment 3
The three great lokas are Bhurloka, the physical world, the earth; Bhuvarloka, the world of becoming, intermediate between the earth and heaven; and Svargaloka, the heaven world. They are made at the beginning of an immense period called a Day of Brahma, and perish at its ending. In Bhurloka, the Prithvi Tattva is the basis of all forms, in seven modifications: solid, liquid, gaseous, radiant, etheric, superetheric, and atomic. In Bhuvarloka the basis is the Apas Tattva, and in Svargaloka the Agni Tattva.

Segment 4
The Jiva has three sheaths, one for each world. The Annamayakosha, the food-sheath, built up out of the food we eat, belongs to the visible earth. The Pranamayakosha, the sheath of Prana, the life-energy, belongs to the invisible part of the earth. The Manomayakosha, the mind-sheath, has two parts: its denser part, where the passions have their seat, belongs to Bhuvarloka, and its finer part, where emotions and thought play, belongs to Svargaloka. Beyond these is the Vijnanamayakosha, the knowledge-sheath, which does not perish at death, and lasts through the series of births and deaths.

Segment 5
At death, the subtle body separates from the solid body, and the Jiva sheds its sheaths one by one, becoming a Preta, then a purified Pitri, then entering Svarga to enjoy the fruit it has stored up. When that fruit is exhausted, the Devas build for it new sheaths, according to its karma, and it is again born into Bhurloka. Life, death, life in the invisible worlds, re-birth, repeated time after time, until at last the Jiva grows weary of these three worlds, turns to meditation and worship and the compassionate helping of the weaker, and becomes a co-worker with Ishvara. Next: Chapter 7 — The Samskaras.


CHAPTER 7 — The Samskaras
=========================

Segment 1
Chapter Seven. The Samskaras. Part Two of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, turns from the great ideas of the first part to the daily religious life of a Hindu, and opens with the ceremonies and rites that mark every stage of his life, and what they are really for.

Segment 2
Every religion prescribes ceremonies, and the book gives them three uses. First, to help the Jiva conquer and purify his sheaths. Second, to bring to his aid superior Intelligences, such as the Devas and the Rishis. And third, to improve the atmosphere around him, so that he can steady and concentrate his mind. To this end are used material objects, gestures, postures and sounds, all carefully arranged. The objects are chosen for their good magnetism. A rosary of tulasi beads is used in the worship of Shri Krishna, and one of rudraksha in the worship of Mahadeva, because each shares the magnetism of the object of worship, and makes a link between that object and the worshipper.

Segment 3
Gestures and postures affect the Pranas, the subdivisions of the life-breath that circulate in the body. Some prevent the magnetism of the body from escaping, and force its currents to move in a way suitable for calm and quiet thought. Sounds are used for all three purposes. Their regular, even vibrations make the vibrations of the subtle body, the Sukshmasharira, regular and even also, and so the Jiva is helped in steadiness of thought and devotion. Sounds thus used are called mantras. A mantra is a definite sequence of sounds, and if the sounds are changed, the vibrations are changed, and the results will be changed. That is why a mantra cannot be translated. A translated mantra is like a sentence in which the letters of the words are jumbled up, and so convey no meaning.

Segment 4
There is another thing to remember about mantra. If the man who utters it is not trying to lead a good life, the vibrations of the mantra will do him harm, not good, for they work on his subtle body and shake it violently. If his resolves are good, however weak they may be, they work with the mantra and cause no conflict. And mantras need not be spoken aloud. Their silent repetition is, indeed, more powerful than audible recitation, for they affect the subtler kinds of matter more than the denser. The book gives the saying: one sound, well-uttered, becometh as the cow of plenty in the Svarga-world.

Segment 5
Among the ceremonies in the life of a Hindu are some that mark the chief stages of his life. In old days ten stood out as the chief. Seven belong to infancy and early childhood; of these the first feeding with solid food, Annaprashanam, is universally observed, and the tonsure with the piercing of the ears, Chudakaranam, almost universally. Then comes the eighth, the Upanayana, when the boy is led to the Guru, who gives him the sacred thread and teaches him the Gayatri, making him a Dvija, twice-born. The student stage follows, closed by the return home, the Samavartanam, and then marriage, Vivaha, marks his entry into the householder life. May the ancient rule be speedily revived. Next: Chapter 8 — Shraddha.


CHAPTER 8 — Shraddha
====================

Segment 1
Chapter Eight. Shraddha. In Part Two of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, the book turns from ideas to customs and rites, and this chapter is about the ceremonies performed for the dead — the rites that help the soul on its journey after death. They are not only for the departed; they bind the living and the dead together, across seven generations.

Segment 2
Shraddha is the name of the ceremonies performed to help the Jiva, the soul, who has put off his visible body in death, by those who remain in this world. The soul that has left its body is called a Preta, and the first part of the rite is called the Preta-kriya. The visible body, the Annamayakosha, is carried to the burning-ground, so that it may dissolve into its constituents as quickly as possible. The ashes are gathered on the third day and thrown into running water, preferably into a sacred stream. Burning, the book says, is the best way of destroying a dead body. When the body is destroyed, the Pranamayakosha, the life-sheath, rapidly disperses, quickened by the mantras used at the cremation.

Segment 3
Why does this matter? So long as the body remains undissolved, the Pranamayakosha hangs round it, held by a magnetic attraction, and the Jiva is thus linked to the earth, which is bad for him. A slowly decaying body in the ground, as in burial, sends out poisonous gases, which is bad for those left behind. The Shraddhas performed after the burning help in a re-arrangement of the materials of the Manomayakosha, the mind-sheath, partly by the magnetic influences of the objects used, and partly by the vibrations set up by the mantras. After a certain time comes the Sapindikarana ceremony, which helps the Jiva to pass from Pretaloka, the world of the Preta, to Pitriloka, the world of the ancestors, where he is enrolled among the Pitris.

Segment 4
Seven generations, the book says, one in Bhurloka, our world, and six in Bhuvarloka, can affect each other. The living help the dead, and the dead, as Pitris, watch over the living. Shraddha, the book defines it, is this: gifts to deserving Brahmanas for the sake of benefit to the Pitris, in the proper times and places and with faith, are known as Shraddha. It should be offered to the Pitris day by day, with corn and water, or even roots and water, bringing them satisfaction. And when the Jiva passes on into Svarga, heaven, he has no further need of the help that Shraddha gives.

Segment 5
The book also speaks of the soul's own road after death. From the five elements a subtler but firm body is born for sinful souls, that they may suffer the punishments awarded by Yama; afterwards it dissolves again into the elements. But if a man has done more Dharma and little Adharma, he goes on to Svarga and enjoys happiness there. The Jiva remains on the path for a year, and by the offering of the sixteen Shraddhas he is helped to dwell in joy with the Pitris. Therefore should the son ever perform the rites for his father. Next: Chapter 9 — Shaucham.


CHAPTER 9 — Shaucham
====================

Segment 1
Chapter Nine. Shaucham. The ninth chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, takes up purity — not as ceremony, but as a law of health and a religious duty.

Segment 2
Shaucha, or Shaucham, is the set of rules for keeping the body pure, and so keeping it healthy and strong. Disease, the book says, is a sign that some law of nature has been disregarded, and the laws of nature are the laws of God. The body is built from the food we eat, the liquids we drink, the air we breathe — and from a constant rain of minute particles falling on us from the people and things around us. Everything we touch gives us some of its particles, and we give it some of ours. So we must take in only pure particles, and drive away the impure ones.

Segment 3
The food we eat must be pure. Fresh leaves and fruits, grains and roots are full of life that is unfolding, and they build the body up; they become impure only when they are stale. All flesh is more or less impure, because its indwelling life has been driven from it and it is ready to decay; a body built on it is more liable to disease, wounds heal less quickly, and fevers run higher. Pure water is necessary to health, and milk is food and drink of the purest kind. But every drink into which alcohol enters is impure, a poison to the brain — and in a hot climate it brings premature decay and early death.

Segment 4
Pure air is as necessary as pure food and drink, for our breath carries out waste particles that must be blown away, not breathed in again. And we must keep the surface of the body clean by frequent washing and bathing — the whole body bathed at least once every day, hands washed before and after food, garments next to the body washed daily. The Hindu, the book says, has joined to his outer ablutions the idea of inner purification: as he washes the outer body, he repeats mantras for the purifying of the inner bodies, weaving his religion into the commonest incidents of daily life. We must be clean, not only for our own sakes, but for the sake of those around us, for a dirty person fills the air with impure particles and poisons the people round him.

Segment 5
Purity reaches deeper than the visible body. The Pranamayakosha, the invisible double of the body, depends on magnetic currents: onions and garlic harm it, alcohol harms it, and so do the vital bodies of others — hence the danger of bad company. And the Manomayakosha depends on the purity of its owner's thoughts and desires, the most fertile source of impurity of all. A man may observe the rules of Shaucha to the last point of strictness, but if he be proud, passionate, harsh, vain, suspicious, he is pouring impurity in faster than any rules can wash it out. In the eyes of the Rishis and the Devas, such a one is ever ashuchi — impure. The chapter closes with the words of Manu: the body is purified by water, the mind by truth, the soul by knowledge and austerity, the reason by wisdom. Next: Chapter 10 — The Five Daily Sacrifices.


CHAPTER 10 — The Five Daily Sacrifices
======================================

Segment 1
Chapter Ten. The Five Daily Sacrifices. In the fourth chapter of Part Two of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, the book turns from the great law of sacrifice to the daily practice of it, and shows how its followers were trained, day by day, to live it.

Segment 2
We have seen that there is a law of Yajna, of sacrifice, and that man's glory is to work with it willingly. Now the book sets out the five great sacrifices to be offered every day. The first is the sacrifice to the Rishis, the sages, or to the Vedas: study and teaching. Every day a man should study some sacred book, a few verses of the Bhagavad Gita or the Hamsa Gita, thinking them over carefully, for carefulness matters more than the amount read. And he should be ready to share what he learns with those more ignorant than himself. All study, the book says, should be a sacrifice, learning in order that we may teach.

Segment 3
The second is the sacrifice to the Devas, the shining ones who work in nature. Its outer form is Homa, the offering into the fire, a recognition of all we owe to their kindly ministry and protection, repaid by giving a share of our possessions for their service. Its inner meaning is the realisation of our relations with the super-physical worlds, and of the interdependence of the worlds. We must learn to be in harmony with nature, in accord with all that lives.

Segment 4
The third is the sacrifice to the Pitris, the ancestors, the offering of water, Tarpana, to the older generations of our family. Its inner meaning is the great debt we owe to the past, to those who toiled and laboured to hand the earth on, improved and enriched, to us, their posterity. No man is truly human, the book says, who does not recognise what he owes to the past. The fourth is the sacrifice to men: hospitality. Every day a true Aryan should feed someone poorer than himself. Its inner meaning is the duty of serving humanity, feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, sheltering the homeless, comforting the sad. Those who are rich are the stewards of the poor.

Segment 5
The fifth is the sacrifice to the Bhutas, to creatures: a little food placed on the ground before the meal, and the remains of the meal left in a suitable place for vagrant men and animals. Its inner meaning is the duty of caring for those beneath us in the human and the lower kingdoms, and the practice of kindness and consideration towards them. So the five daily sacrifices teach a man his relations with all around him, with his superiors, his equals, and his inferiors. They turn the wheel of life in accord with the will of Ishvara, and they teach each of us that we are not an isolated unit, but a part of a great whole, a cell in a vast body. Next: Chapter 11 — Worship.


CHAPTER 11 — Worship
====================

Segment 1
Chapter Eleven. Worship. The fifth chapter of Part Two of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, turns from duty to devotion. The five daily sacrifices are not enough, the book says. The truly religious man longs for something more: to come into conscious relation with Ishvara, the Lord of the Universe, the great Life of which his own life is a part. That longing finds its satisfaction in worship.

Segment 2
Even Vyasa, the book tells us, knew this longing. Master of the final truth, he had written the Mahabharata and the Brahma-sutras for the instruction of men, yet still he found no peace of mind. Narada counselled him to sing the praise of Ishvara, and this he did in the Bhagavata, winning thereby the peace he had not gained before. Worship, the book says, is the expression of love to the Supreme, of reverence towards Him, of aspiration to reach Him in conscious communion, of longing to be united with Him, to feel the unity of the individual self with the supreme Self.

Segment 3
Worship may take many forms: praise of His perfection, prayer rooted in a sense of imperfection, appeal to His love, recognition of His power, meditation on His nature, intense longing for His unveiling, according to the temperament and stage of the worshipper. But in peasant or philosopher, the root-longing is the same. The All, the Unconditioned, is never an object of worship; attributes are needed, on which the mind can be fixed. So it is Saguna Brahman, Ishvara, whom all prayers reach. He may be adored as Shiva or Vishnu, Durga or Lakshmi, Ganesha or Sarasvati, or as an Avatara — Rama, Krishna, or Buddha — but under whatever name and form, it is Ishvara who is worshipped.

Segment 4
All these are Forms, the book insists, and Ishvara is One. The worshipper worships not the Form but the Lord in the Form, as a wife loves her husband, not the clothes he wears, though even those may be dear for the sake of the wearer. This is why the quarrels of different religions, and of different sects in the same religion, are so foolish and so ignorant: all are worshipping the same Ishvara, and the differences are only differences of names. Puja is the general simple form of worship: a picture or image, mantras recited, flowers offered, water poured out, and in these outer forms the inner love finds expression, then rises beyond the forms to the Object thus served.

Segment 5
Upasana includes many forms of worship, among them meditation and the daily Sandhya, which should be performed by all followers of the Sanatana Dharma. And the chapter closes with the words of the Bhagavad Gita: "However men approach Me, even so do I welcome them, for the path men take from every side is mine." And again: "The Lord dwelleth in the hearts of all beings. Flee unto Him for shelter with all thy being; by His grace thou shalt obtain supreme peace and the everlasting dwelling place." Next: Chapter 12 — The Four Ashramas.


CHAPTER 12 — The Four Ashramas
==============================

Segment 1
Chapter Twelve. The Four Ashramas. In this chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, the book turns from the soul's long journey through many births to the stages of a single life, from birth to death. It asks a simple question: how should a life be lived, in order, so that each age gets its own work and its own joy?

Segment 2
The chapter opens with the character of the Hindu nation in old days: order and balance. Sanatana Dharma stamped those qualities on the people, and Shri Krishna said, "Equilibrium is called Yoga." The Atma, the Self, is in no haste, being Eternal. He unfolds his powers in due order, as Ishvara orders the seed, the root, the stem, the leaves, the flower, and the fruit, each in its season. But the Jivatma in man pushes irregularly, grasping at the things of one stage while yet in another, and so life is robbed of its serenity. To set this right, the great Rishis marked out four resting-places in a single life, the Ashramas: Brahmacharya, studentship; Garhasthya, the household; Vanaprastha, the forest; and Sannyasa, renunciation. Each stage has its own duties and its own pleasures, and none must grasp at another's.

Segment 3
The life of the student begins with the Upanayana ceremony, his second birth. He must be hardy and simple in his habits: rising early, bathing, eating moderately of plain food, taking plenty of exercise. The book contrasts him with the boy who lies late in bed, over-eats on sweetmeats, and lounges on soft pillows: the one is alert, strong, and vigorous; the other heavy, flabby, and soon diseased. He must also strive for industry, obedience, humility, and serviceableness, and be chaste in thought and act, for the boy who does not think impurity will not act impurity. The very name Brahmachari has come to mean the celibate. Student-marriage, against the ancient law, brings premature age, weakness, disease, and race-decay.

Segment 4
The household stage is entered at marriage, and of all the Ashramas it is the most important, for it supports all the others. The welfare of family and nation depends on the householder, and a good husband, a good father, a good master, and a good citizen is the noblest of men. The home is the school of unselfishness, compassion, tenderness, and charity. When some well-born youths abandoned their homes too early for the forest, Indra pitied them and, in the form of a golden bird, taught them: "Follow the household life! It is the field for the cultivation of virtues. It is sacred." To eat the remnant of food left after helping the needy is to eat the true remnant of sacrifice. Plucking unripe fruit deprives the eater of its sweetness.

Segment 5
When the householder sees his sons able to bear his duties, when the signs of age appear and his children's children are round him, the time has come to retire with his wife to a quiet, secluded life of study and counselling the younger. Finally, in old age, a man may enter the fourth Ashrama, passing his last days in meditation and worship, till he enters willingly the life beyond death. The book closes with the words of the Gita: he who performs his duty, independent of the fruit of action, he is a Sannyasi, he is a Yogi. Next: Chapter 13 — The Four Castes.


CHAPTER 13 — The Four Castes
============================

Segment 1
Chapter Thirteen. The Four Castes. This chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, turns from the soul's journey to the shape of society. In the soul's long pilgrimage through many births, the book says, there are four distinctly marked stages, called of old varnas, or colours. And Sanatana Dharma made those four stages the framework of its social polity.

Segment 2
The first stage is the soul's infancy, childhood, and youth. It is unfolding obedience, serviceableness, and patience, with few responsibilities, and its duties are summed up in one word: service. Such souls, the book says, were born into the caste of Shudras, labourers, artisans, servants, and manual workers of every kind. The second stage covers the first half of manhood, fit to gain wealth, to organise labour, and to administer great possessions, the traders, bankers, and organisers of industry, born into the caste of Vaishyas.

Segment 3
The third stage makes the second half of manhood, when responsibilities widen to include the nation. He is called to legislate and to rule, to protect and to regulate, not to aggrandise himself, kings, judges, legislators, and warriors, born into the Kshattriya caste, bearing the burden of kingship and of national defence. The fourth stage is the serene age, when earthly things have lost their charm, and the soul becomes counsellor and friend and helper of all, priests, teachers, authors, scientists, and philosophers, the Brahmanas, who gave much and needed little.

Segment 4
In the early days, the book says, the outer caste coincided with the stage, and society was contented and progressive. But in these later days, varnasankara, caste-confusion, has come, as Arjuna truly foresaw in his distress on Kurukshetra. Young souls are born into bodies of other stages, and older souls fret against uncongenial surroundings. The evil has grown from each caste grasping at the work of the others, and thinking more of the rights its caste gives than of the duties it imposes, until antagonisms have replaced mutual service and good will. Yet the Rigveda says: the Brahmana was His mouth; the Rajanya was made His two arms; His two thighs the Vaishya; the Shudra was born from His two feet, one body, four parts.

Segment 5
But at least this much, the chapter says, every boy can do: diligently practise the virtues characteristic of his caste, and avoid pride, vanity, and grasping at privileges. Let the Shudra be remarkable for industry, fidelity, and serviceableness; the Vaishya for diligence, discretion, and charity; the Kshattriya for courage, generosity, and vigour; the Brahmana for patience, purity, learning, truthfulness, and self-sacrifice. And conduct alone, the Mahabharata says, is the cause of being twice-born, not birth. Next: Chapter 14, Ethical Science, what it is.


CHAPTER 14 — Ethical Science, what it is
========================================

Segment 1
Chapter Fourteen. Ethical Science, what it is. Part Three of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, opens its Ethical Teachings with a plain question: what makes a list of rules into a science? The chapter answers by defining its two words — science, and ethical — and from those two definitions it builds a whole teaching about right conduct, about Dharma, and about the happiness that right conduct brings.

Segment 2
A science, the book says, is a definitely arranged body of knowledge — facts related to each other, set in their proper order, with the principles underlying those relations known. A mere collection of facts does not make a science. Ethical means that which relates to conduct: the way rational beings should behave towards each other, and towards other creatures. So Ethical Science is not a list of virtues and vices. It is a definite, systematically arranged series of facts of conduct, in their proper relations to each other, with the principles of those relations understood. Another word for Ethics is morality — right conduct. And to know what is right, we must understand something about man, and about his surroundings.

Segment 3
The object of morality, the chapter says, is to secure the welfare of all beings — by showing men, through the science of Conduct, how to live in harmony with each other, and with their surroundings. Ishvara is Love, and He wills the happiness of His universe, and is gradually bringing about universal happiness. That does not mean a right thing is always pleasant at the time, or a wrong thing always unpleasant. It means the conduct that leads to lasting happiness — to the bliss of union with Ishvara, to Moksha in the end — is good. As the wheels of the cart follow the ox, so misery follows sin, and so also happiness follows righteousness.

Segment 4
The fruit of wrongdoing may taste sweet, but it is poison, bringing pain afterwards. The boy who does wrong is like the ignorant child who plucks and eats the poison-berries, and writhes in pain in the evening. The teacher of morality must mark every sin Poison. Then comes Achara, good conduct. Achara is the mark of Dharma, higher than all teachings is Achara, the Mahabharata says. From Achara is Dharma born, and Dharma enhances life. Dharma is defined by what it does: it upholds and holds together; it brings the well-being of all beings; it makes beings harmless. He who is the friend of all beings, intent on the welfare of all in act, thought and speech — he knoweth Dharma.

Segment 5
The chapter closes with the four ends of life: Dharma, Artha, and Kama — religion, wealth, and pleasure — and Moksha, emancipation. The wise man serves Dharma, Artha and Kama evenly, dividing his time between them, on the path of outgoing. But attachment is the great frustrater of all four, and the other three are ever haunted by the fear of Death, the Ender. Moksha alone is the truly ultimate end, the highest good, on the path of return. Next: Chapter 15 — The Foundation of Ethics as given by Religion.


CHAPTER 15 — The Foundation of Ethics as given by Religion
==========================================================

Segment 1
Chapter Fifteen. The Foundation of Ethics as given by Religion. In Part Three of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, the book turns from what religion teaches to how we should live — and it begins by laying one foundation stone: the Self is One. It asks a simple question: if the Self is one in all beings, what does that mean for how we treat one another?

Segment 2
There are indeed many selves in appearance, the book says, but they are all portions or reflections of the One. Their separateness is temporary; their unity is eternal. Many pots may be dipped into a tank, but the water that fills each is the same water. Many selves are dipped into the ocean of existence, but the life that fills each is the same life. This primary truth of religion, the book says, is the foundation of Ethics.

Segment 3
But unity alone is not enough, for in unity there is neither "I" nor "you", and ethics deals with relations between "I" and "you". So we must also recognise the diversity of the Not-Self: innumerable bodies and innumerable minds, each holding a part or reflection of the One Self, coming into relations with each other. Right relations can never exist until each mind and body acts on the principle that they are at root one — that what helps all is the only thing that really helps each, and that what injures one really injures all.

Segment 4
In hurting another, the book says, we are really hurting ourselves. If a hand cut the foot belonging to its own body, the blood would flow from the foot, not from the hand — but presently the hand would grow weak, for the blood circulates in the whole body and there is only one supply for all parts. So also with men: if one man wounds another, the wounder suffers as much as the wounded, only the suffering takes longer in making itself felt. This is the foundation of right conduct, as seen by Reason.

Segment 5
Boys, the book says, must at first take moral precepts on the authority of great Sages and Saints, as taught in the Shastras, because they have not the power nor the time to think them out for themselves. But in manhood they can verify these precepts by applying their Reason to them. And the chapter closes with the words of Shri Krishna: "I am the Self, O Gudakesha, seated in the heart of all beings; I am the beginning, the middle, and also the end of beings." Let this principle sink deeply into every heart. Next: Chapter 16 — Right and Wrong.


CHAPTER 16 — Right and Wrong
============================

Segment 1
Chapter Sixteen. Right and Wrong. The third chapter of Part Three, the Ethical Teachings of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, takes up two words that are in everybody's mouth — right and wrong — and asks what they really mean. The answer it gives is simple to state and hard to live: right is whatever moves us towards unity, and wrong is whatever holds us apart.

Segment 2
The chapter pictures evolution as two paths. First comes the Pravritti Marga, the path of going forth: the soul builds itself into a separate individual, taking all it can from outside and making a strong intellect, a powerful I. Then comes the turning. The soul learns it is only part of a much greater I, Ishvara, and begins the Nivritti Marga, the path of return: seeing the unity under the diversity, giving to all who are weaker, sharing with others what it has gathered. To work with the Will of Ishvara is Right; to work against it is Wrong.

Segment 3
The world, the book says, stands at the turning point between the two paths. Most people are still on the path of going forth, but their higher evolution lies along the path of return. So right conduct is the desires, thoughts and actions that help oneself and others tread the Nivritti Marga — all that lessens separateness and leads towards unity is Right; all that increases separateness and leads away from unity is Wrong. But morality is relative: for animals, savages and backward souls whose individuality is still very weak, separateness has still to be aimed at, and what is right for the more advanced is not yet right for them.

Segment 4
Bhishma said to Yudhishthira, "Subtle is morality." Scripture must be applied with careful judgment, and, as the sage Ushanas said, scriptures are no scriptures if they cannot stand the test of reason. In a long famine, the Rishi Vishvamitra took forbidden meat and was justified; forgiveness befits the sannyasi, but it does not always befit the king, who may forgive injuries to himself, but not the slightest wrong done to his meanest subject. The king must cherish the poor and punish the wicked, lest men prowl unchecked, like wolves, murdering the weaker. Then the simple rules: to give joy to another is righteousness; to give pain is sin. Let no man do unto another what he would not have done to himself.

Segment 5
Karma, too, is of two kinds: Pravritta karma, done under personal desire for gain, and Nivritta karma, done without such desire, under the guidance of perfected knowledge — and that rises beyond the five elements. The Bhagavad Gita closes the chapter: worship Him in your own karma, and you shall win perfection; better your own dharma, though destitute of merits, than another's well executed. "Ishvara dwelleth in the hearts of all beings, O Arjuna. Flee unto Him for shelter with all thy being; by His grace thou shalt obtain supreme peace, the everlasting dwelling place." Next: Chapter 17 — The Standard of Ethics.


CHAPTER 17 — The Standard of Ethics
===================================

Segment 1
Chapter Seventeen. The Standard of Ethics. In Part Three of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, the book turns from what is true to what is right, and gives the student a single standard by which every action can be judged.

Segment 2
We have seen, the book says, that the general standard by which actions should be judged at the present stage of evolution is Union. For the majority of men, each action can be tested by asking two questions. Does it promote union? Does it promote separateness? If the answer to the first question is yes, the action is Right. If the answer to the second question is yes, the action is Wrong. This is why morality was described in the first chapter as showing men how to live in harmony with each other and with their surroundings. To live in harmony is to promote union.

Segment 3
The book turns to Shri Krishna, who speaks in the Bhagavad Gita of Divine and Asuric properties. The Divine qualities are those which promote union. Among them are fearlessness, cleanness of life, steadfastness in the yoga of wisdom, alms-giving, self-restraint and sacrifice, study of the Shastras, austerity and straightforwardness, harmlessness, truthfulness, absence of wrath, renunciation, peacefulness, compassion to living beings, mildness, modesty, vigour, forgiveness, fortitude, purity, and absence of envy and pride. Such virtues draw men together, the book says, because they are based on the knowledge that all selves are one. The Asuric qualities do the opposite: hypocrisy, arrogance and conceit, wrath, harshness and unwisdom. The Asuric man is full of ahamkara, egoism and selfishness, and his qualities drive men apart.

Segment 4
Let the young student grasp firmly this distinction between Right and Wrong, the book says, and apply it to the guidance of his conduct. In later study he will add much to his thoughts on Right and Wrong, will learn many details, and have to solve many complexities. But this principle, this standard, will remain unaltered, for it is based on evolution, and is in accord with the will of Ishvara. The chapter then quotes the Manusmriti: of all these things, the knowledge of the Self is the highest. It is the foremost of the sciences, for immortality is attained thereby. He who thus seeth the Self in all beings, by his own Self, realiseth the equality of all, and attaineth to the supreme state of Brahman.

Segment 5
So the standard of ethics is simple, and it never changes. Whatever draws selves together is Right. Whatever drives them apart is Wrong. And behind the standard stands a single truth: the Self is in all beings, and all beings are one Self. To see that, and to live by it, is the whole of ethics. Next: Chapter 18 — Virtues and their foundation.


CHAPTER 18 — Virtues and their foundation
=========================================

Segment 1
Chapter Eighteen. Virtues and their foundation. In Part Three of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, the book asks what a virtue really is, and where virtues come from. We have already seen, it says, that mutual sacrifice, another name for mutual service, is enjoined by the Sanatana Dharma, because it promotes union. And there is another way the Dharma teaches us to do right: by explaining the three debts that we owe.

Segment 2
The three debts are these. The debt to the Rishis, paid chiefly in the Brahmacharya Ashrama, by study and by service of the teacher. The debt to the Pitris, the Ancestors, paid chiefly in the Garhasthya Ashrama, by rearing a family and by charity. And the debt to the Devas, paid chiefly in the Vanaprastha Ashrama, by sacrifice and meditation. A debt is something we owe: we have received, and should repay. This payment of what is due is called the discharge of duty. The discharge of a duty is called a virtue; the non-discharge of a duty is called a vice. A virtuous man recognises his duties and fulfils them; a vicious man does not recognise them, nor fulfil them.

Segment 3
Virtues have also been called, by Bhishma, forms of Truth, because Truth is that which is Real, indeed, as Bhishma says, the eternal Brahman. All the laws of Nature are expressions of Truth, and work with perfect accuracy and changelessness. And the greatest Truth of all is the unity of the Self amidst the diversity of the Not-Self. The form which this great Truth takes in Ethical Science is that each one of us should feel for other selves as for himself. And so feeling, we must always tell the truth, because to tell a falsehood to another is to regard him as separate from, or even opposed to, oneself. Truthfulness promotes union, while untruthfulness increases separateness.

Segment 4
The chapter tells story after story of men who kept their word, whatever it cost. Shri Krishna promised not to carry arms on Kurukshetra, and when He ran towards Bhishma with uplifted whip to help Arjuna, Arjuna refused His help because of His promise. Yudhishthira, though almost despairing of victory, would not take His help for the same reason; and when for a moment he forgot his truthfulness and told a lie to the undoing of Drona, his war-chariot sank to the ground. Prahlada gave his Shila, his character, to Indra disguised as a Brahmana, though he feared it meant his own ruin, because he had pledged his word. And Bhishma, when his step-mother prayed him to take the throne against his promise, answered: I would renounce the three worlds, the empire of heaven, or anything that may be still greater, but truth never will I renounce.

Segment 5
Karna gave away the natural armour no weapon could pierce, hewing it off with his own hand, though he knew it meant his life. King Dasharatha kept the two boons he had promised Kaikeyi, though granting them meant his death. And Bali gave Vamana the land of three paces, and offered his own head for the third, though he knew Vamana was Vishnu, his enemy. Above and beyond all else, therefore, a boy should strive for truthfulness. For, as the book says, Truth is the Sanatana Dharma. Truth is the eternal Brahman. Next: Chapter 19 — Bliss and Emotions.


CHAPTER 19 — Bliss and Emotions
===============================

Segment 1
Chapter Nineteen. Bliss and Emotions. In Part Three, Ethical Teachings, of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, the book turns from what man is to how he should live, and it begins with bliss, the third aspect of Ishvara himself. For man, as the child of Ishvara, carries Thought, Power and Bliss within him, and this chapter asks where bliss is to be found.

Segment 2
Ishvara is Thought, Power and Bliss, and man, as His child, shares those three. But when the Jivatma, the separated Self, is wrapped in dense matter, its Bliss turns outward, seeking satisfaction by uniting itself with the outer world. The impulses outward are called desires. When desire unites the Jivatma to an object that gives pleasure, so that he wishes to be united to it again, the emotion is love, or liking. When it unites him to an object that gives pain, so that he wishes to avoid it, the emotion is hate, or dislike. Love is attraction; hate is repulsion.

Segment 3
The chapter quotes the Katha Upanishad: the Self-born pierced the senses outwards, therefore the Jiva looks outwards, away from the inner Self. And the Chandogya Upanishad: when the Jiva obtains pleasure, then he engages in action; never does he enter on action without having obtained pleasure. But the book draws a sharp line. That which is infinite, that is happiness; there is no happiness in the finite. Where the Self sees no other than the Self, that is the Infinite; where it sees another, that is the finite. The infinite is immortal; the finite is mortal.

Segment 4
So the Jivatma must think over his likes and dislikes, and train himself to direct his emotions rightly. Emotions guided by reason, in accordance with the will of Ishvara, develop into virtues, and the culture of the emotions is the ethical development of man. The Bhagavad Gita warns that affection and aversion abide in the senses, and are obstructors of the path. But the disciplined self, moving among sense objects with senses free from attraction and repulsion, mastered by the self, goes to peace. Desire and anger, the Gita says, arise out of Rajas, and following desire alone brings neither perfection, nor happiness, nor the highest goal.

Segment 5
As a man cultivates love, he unites himself to an ever-widening circle: the family, the community, the nation, the race, loving them as himself. That love is the Bliss in his nature finding satisfaction in union. Evolution itself is leading us towards union, the will of Ishvara guiding the separated selves towards union with each other and with Himself. In this union is Bliss; therefore the Right and the Happy are inseparable. Brahman is bliss. Next: Chapter 20 — Self-regarding Virtues.


CHAPTER 20 — Self-regarding Virtues
===================================

Segment 1
Chapter Twenty. Self-regarding Virtues. In this chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, ethics turns inward, to the virtues a person owes first to himself. Before we can live rightly with others, the book says, we must first rule ourselves.

Segment 2
We have seen that the Jivatma is related to all around him, but he is most closely related to his own bodies, his koshas, the parts of the Not-Self he has most closely united to himself. Unless he is in harmonious relations with these, he can hardly hope to be in harmonious relations with anything further away. While he is young, these bodies master him and lead him into trouble. As he grows older, he begins to master them, and many a hard fight he has before he develops the great virtue called self-control: the control of the lower selves by the higher Self, of the bodies by the Jivatma.

Segment 3
Manu, the great law-giver, says that action has three forces behind it, and each must be mastered. Action is born of mind, speech and body. The mind must be conquered first, and this is the hardest task of all, for the mind runs after the objects of desire, swayed by longings to possess what promises pleasure, and becomes the slave of the emotions. The Jivatma must free the mind from this slavery and make it master of the senses. As Manu says, in conquering the mind, the two sets of five become conquered: the ten Indriyas, the five senses of knowledge and the five of action.

Segment 4
The control of speech is the next step: we must think before we speak, for hasty speech leads to endless troubles. Arjuna was often very hasty in his speech, and so got himself into many difficulties, as in his vow to kill Jayadratha before sunset, which needed Shri Krishna to shroud the sun. His failure to keep a hasty vow led to his death on the Great Journey. He who has conquered his tongue is near to self-mastery. Thirdly, the physical body must be controlled. Shri Krishna calls the austerity of the body the worship of the Devas, the twice-born, the gurus and the wise, purity, straightforwardness, chastity and harmlessness. Youth is the time for conquering the body, for the body is a creature of habit, and a little perseverance makes it give way.

Segment 5
Selfish desire is a source of evil and sorrow: peace arises from the absence of desires, not from their gratification. Manki, greedy of wealth, was ever disappointed, until the loss of his last pair of calves opened his heart, and he sang: He that desires happiness must renounce desire. Thus Manki lost a little, and gained all. King Yayati borrowed his son Puru's youth for a thousand years to exhaust the pleasures of the senses, but his craving only grew, until he saw that desire ceases not with satisfaction but with frustration, and retired chanting: Desire may not be quenched by enjoyment of its objects; it only increases manifold therewith, as fire with libations of butter. And harmlessness is the highest Dharma, Bhishma taught: we should injure nothing. Bhima's boyish rough play planted a smouldering fire of dislike that grew into the blazing hate that consumed Kaurava and Pandava alike. The triple control of mind, speech and body results in righteousness. All the virtues have their root in pure Love, and have Bliss for their fruit; all the vices have their root in personal Hate, and their fruit is Misery. Next: Chapter 21 — Virtues and Vices in relation to Superiors.


CHAPTER 21 — Virtues and Vices in relation to Superiors
=======================================================

Segment 1
Chapter Twenty-One. Virtues and Vices in relation to Superiors. This chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, turns from ethics in general to how love should shape our conduct toward those above us. Love, the book says, is the root of the virtues, for it prompts us to sacrifice for others, while hate is the root of the vices, for it grasps at things for ourselves alone. Our superiors are five: God, the Sovereign, our Parents, our Teachers, and the Aged.

Segment 2
Love toward God shows itself as reverence, devotion, worship, and submission to His will. Bhishma, at the Rajasuya sacrifice, bids them offer the first worship to Shri Krishna, and dying, he thinks of Krishna in mind, word, and act, closing with the thousand names of Vasudeva. Prahlada prays to Hari though his father threatens to kill him, and Narasimha bursts from the pillar to save him. Dhruva worships with such fervour that Hari gives him the pole-star for a throne. And Rama, excluded from the throne, stays utterly unshaken, knowing the Real amid changing unrealities.

Segment 3
Those who set themselves against the Lord fall. Ravana, monarch of Lanka, fell because he thought himself a rival of Ishvara. Jarasandha was slain by Bhima, Shishupala before the discus of the Lord he denied, and Duryodhana perished for rejecting Shri Krishna's counsels. Loyalty to the head of the State is insisted on just as strongly. The Pandavas bring all the wealth they win in war to Yudhishthira's feet, fighting for their King, not for themselves. And when the people follow the exiled Yudhishthira, he bids them return and obey their proper ruler, since only thus can they secure the general prosperity.

Segment 4
The King, the book says, exists to uphold righteousness, not to act capriciously. All creatures rest upon righteousness, and righteousness rests upon the King. Patriotism and public spirit belong with loyalty, for King and Country are one object of love. Public spirit makes a man feel the successes and sufferings of his country as his own, protect the weak, uphold the law, and refuse unfair profit at the community's cost. To parents is due the most complete obedience. Rama, told of his exile, answers at once that he will carry out his father's word, saying there is no power in him to transgress his father's order, and later, what my father hath commanded must not be made untrue.

Segment 5
The fowler who knows Brahman tells the Brahmana Kaushika that his parents are the idols he worships, like the three sacred fires, and that no virtue is higher than honouring them. Bhishma gives up throne and marriage so his father may wed Satyavati, and wins the boon of dying only at his own will. But Duryodhana's disobedience to his parents precipitated the war that destroyed his house. The teacher stands beside father and mother as a third great object of reverence, and the aged close the list, for their wisdom is the fruit of long experience, and saluting them brings length of life, knowledge, fame, and strength. Next: Chapter 22 — Virtues and Vices in relation to Equals.


CHAPTER 22 — Virtues and Vices in relation to Equals
====================================================

Segment 1
Chapter Twenty-Two. Virtues and Vices in relation to Equals. This chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, turns from our duties to those above us, to the equals who surround us on every side: in the home, and in the world outside it. What virtues should we develop, and what vices avoid, to make our home and our outer relations harmonious and happy?

Segment 2
First, the home, for pure and happy homes, the book says, are the foundation of prosperous states and successful nations. Husband and wife, says Manu, are the same; they are one, not two, and love makes the two into one: love protective, sheltering and tender on the husband's side, love yielding, sweet and devoted on the wife's. Rama and Sita are the ideal: they enjoy life's happinesses together and suffer its sorrows together, take counsel together in every perplexity, and share together every difficulty. When exile comes, Sita will not be left behind. Thorns would touch her skin like soft linen, dust would be as sandal-powder, so long as she was by her husband's side. Thy company is heaven, thy absence hell.

Segment 3
Then Savitri, who won her husband back from Yama, the King of Death. Told that Satyavan would die within a year, she marries him all the same, saying, but once can a person give herself away, and I have said it once. She fasts three days, follows Yama as he carries her husband's soul away, and wins boon after boon with wise and gentle words: her father-in-law's sight, his kingdom, a hundred sons for her father and a hundred for herself, until Yama grants her husband's life itself. Even Yama, King of Death, the book says, is weaker than a pure wife's faithful love. And Damayanti, too, clings to Nala through exile and hunger, for in every sorrow there is no such medicine as a loving and faithful wife.

Segment 4
Brotherly love is shown in the Ramayana, where Lakshmana is like Rama's life itself: they would not sleep apart, nor apart engage in sport, and Lakshmana follows Rama into the forest and stands waking on watch while Rama sleeps. When Lakshmana lies senseless, arrow-pierced, Rama cries, without thee, life and victory, nay, Sita's self, are worthless. The Pandavas show the same union: never with warring interests, never leading separated lives, all their wealth gained for Yudhishthira, and he regarding their joys and sorrows as his own. Even in heaven, Yudhishthira will not stay without his brothers, crying, that is heaven where my brothers are; this is not heaven for me. For stronger than hell is love, and fidelity than pain.

Segment 5
Outside the family, hospitality stands in the first rank. A poor Brahmana, living on grains gathered in famine, gives his scanty barley, share by share, to a guest, until the guest is revealed as Dharma himself, and a mongoose rolling in the grains left uneaten turns half to gold. And forgiveness marks a truly noble nature: Rama forgets a hundred injuries and remembers one benefit, and Vidura, driven away with insult by his brother, returns at once when called, saying simply, O King, I have forgiven. So the book teaches gentle speech to equals, for there are no riches like compassion, friendliness, charity, and sweet speech. Next: Chapter 23 — Virtues and Vices in relation to Inferiors.


CHAPTER 23 — Virtues and Vices in relation to Inferiors
=======================================================

Segment 1
Chapter Twenty-Three. Virtues and Vices in relation to Inferiors. As we go further into the world, says Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, we meet many people below us in some way: younger than ourselves, less educated, poorer, or lower in social rank. What virtues should we cultivate toward them, and what vices should we avoid, if our relations with them are to be harmonious?

Segment 2
The first and most obvious of these relations is with our youngers, and the best examples are seen in parents and their children. Tenderness, compassion, gentleness, kindness: how strongly these virtues shine out in loving parents, and how happy they make the home. A father and mother suffer in their children's sufferings and are glad in their pleasures. The book tells of Surabhi, the celestial mother of cows, who wept before Indra not for herself, but for her feeble son, beaten at the plough. "For each of those thousands that suffers thus, I weep," she said, "and I weep more for the one that is weaker than for the others." So too Dasharatha loved Rama, Kunti wailed as her five sons went into exile, and Arjuna grieved for his son Abhimanyu, above all that he had not been there to protect him.

Segment 3
This duty of protecting the weak is incarnated in the righteous king, and it is the fulfilment of this duty which awakens the loyalty of his subjects. "To protect his subjects is the cream of kingly duties," says Bhishma, and a king should behave toward his subjects as a mother toward the child of her womb. Yudhishthira would not enter heaven without the faithful dog that had followed him through the desert, saying it would be a sin to abandon one so devoted. King Shibi gave flesh from his own body, and at last his whole body, to save a dove that had sought his refuge from a hawk. And Rantideva, after fasting for forty-eight days, gave away his first meal, share by share, and finally his last drink of water to a suffering outcaste, praying only that he might suffer for all beings, that they might live without sorrow.

Segment 4
But there is a danger in showing tenderness and protection to those weaker than ourselves: the vice of pride. Pride arises from ahamkara, the sense of separateness of "I" and "you," which thinks more of the fact that "I am helping this weaker one" than of sharing what is really a common store. By dwelling on one's own usefulness, pride is awakened, and quickly ruins the good work performed. Even the very highest fall beneath its sway. The sage Narayana, proud of his power, was drawn into a promise of marriage; Vishvamitra's greed for Vashishtha's cow led to a war that changed the history of the land; Indra, who would not rise to greet his teacher, lost his throne; Nahusha, set to rule heaven in Indra's place, grew proud and fell to earth as a serpent; and Bali lost the goddess of prosperity when pride entered his heart.

Segment 5
Sometimes, the chapter says, the inferior may save his superior from falling into sin through wrath and pride. Chirakarin, whose name means acting slowly, thought long when his angry father ordered him to slay his mother, and by his patience did his father's real will, not his hasty order, saving his father from a grievous sin. So the teaching closes as it began, with gentleness: created beings must be instructed without giving them pain, and sweet and gentle speech must be used by a superior who desires to fulfil the sacred law. Next: Chapter 24 — The Re-action of Virtues and Vices on each other.


CHAPTER 24 — The Re-action of Virtues and Vices on each other
=============================================================

Segment 1
Chapter Twenty-Four. The Re-action of Virtues and Vices on each other. The last chapter of Sanatana Dharma, the 1916 textbook of the Central Hindu College, gathers everything the book has taught about right conduct into one practical question: how do our virtues and vices act upon other people? For we do not live alone: every feeling we show awakens the same feeling in those around us.

Segment 2
By showing love to others, we awaken love in them; by showing hate, we awaken hate. An angry word brings an angry reply, and that brings a still more angry retort. Gentle words bring gentle words in reply; kindness arouses kindness, and good deeds cause good deeds in others. So we can meet a wrong feeling with a right one, instead of being swept into it. When Draupadi urged King Yudhishthira to attack the Kurus who had cheated and ruined him, the wise king answered that returning evil for evil could only continue misery. If the injured returned their injuries, he said, fathers would slay sons and sons fathers, and how could birth take place in a world filled with anger? For the birth of creatures is due to peace.

Segment 3
The book shows this working in story after story. Kaushalya, grieving for her exiled son Rama, spoke angry words to her husband King Dasharatha. He did not answer bitterly. With folded hands, he begged her forgiveness, and her anger vanished, giving place to humility and remorse. Rama awakened trust in Lakshmana's angry breast: when Lakshmana was sure Bharata was marching to slay them, Rama answered with love for his brother, and Lakshmana's wrath gave place to shame. And Yudhishthira, blamed again by his own brothers in exile, met their harsh words with gentle humility, saying that virtue is better than life itself, and that kingdom, sons, fame and wealth do not come up to one-sixteenth part of truth.

Segment 4
But the same law works for evil. Thoughtless ridicule arouses hatred: when Bhima laughed at Duryodhana slipping in Yudhishthira's hall, the insult festered into vengeance, the gambling match, the exile, and at last the battle of Kurukshetra. Evil returned with evil only lengthens the chain of misery. In anger, Jamadagni ordered his son Rama of the Axe to slay his own mother, and that deed led to murder upon murder, until Rama vowed the slaughter of a whole caste. Only forgiveness can cut such a chain. Even Duryodhana was once softened: treated with unfailing patience by his difficult guest Durvasa, he won a boon. Yet when Yudhishthira later rescued him from his enemies, the very kindness became a new offence, and he hated only the more.

Segment 5
So the book ends with practice, not theory. Even when another's anger arouses anger in us, we may check it and refuse it expression in word, look, or gesture, until the feeling dies away. Avoid bad company, whose unkind thoughts work upon us, and keep the mind busy with pure and high thoughts, so we influence others for good. For he who is not angry with the angry is a physician unto both, and this world is upheld by forgiveness. May all rejoice everywhere. Peace to all beings.
